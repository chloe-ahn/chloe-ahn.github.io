I use micro data to study household financial and housing decisions, and what they mean for the macroeconomy. My fields are household finance, housing, public economics, inequality and measurement. I ask how economic conditions and public policy shape these decisions over the life cycle, and who bears the costs. I also ask how these decisions, added up across households, move asset prices and long-term interest rates and change what monetary and fiscal policy can achieve.

Working Paper

Priced Out: Housing Costs, Generational Wealth Gaps, and Youth Welfare

PDF · Latest version October 2026

Abstract

Each US birth cohort from 1940 to 1980 has owned less housing than the one before it. In the Panel Study of Income Dynamics, ownership among household heads aged 25 to 34 fell from 53 percent in the 1940 cohort to 31 percent in the 1980 cohort. I estimate a life-cycle model of saving, tenure and housing quality on the 1940 cohort, then hold preferences fixed and let each later cohort face the house prices, interest rates and earnings of its own time. The changing environment alone produces a fall in ownership of about the size seen in the data. House prices account for the largest part of the ownership gap, while earnings account for somewhat more of the welfare loss, which falls hardest on households with low income and little wealth.
